Why Euclid is logarithmic — Lamé’s theorem

The number of steps of Euclid's algorithm on $(a,b)$ with $a>b$ is at most $5\log_{10} b$. The worst case is exactly when $(a,b)$ are consecutive Fibonacci numbers. This is the first historical result connecting algorithm analysis with number theory.

Closed-form Fibonacci (Binet)

$F_n = \dfrac{\varphi^n - \psi^n}{\sqrt 5}$ where $\varphi=(1+\sqrt 5)/2,\ \psi=(1-\sqrt 5)/2$. Computing $\varphi^n$ by fast exponentiation gives $O(\log n)$ arithmetic ops, but floating-point loses accuracy for large $n$. Matrix-power method $\binom{F_{n+1}}{F_n}=\binom{1\,1}{1\,0}^n\binom{1}{0}$ also runs in $O(\log n)$ multiplications.

Sequential search variations

• Sentinel search: append the key at the end; the loop body removes one comparison per iteration (still $O(n)$ but ~2× faster in practice).
• On sorted data we can stop early when $A[i]>$ key, but this does not change the asymptotic bound — if you have sorted data, prefer Binary Search ($O(\log n)$, Unit 3).

Adaptive sorting and “number of inversions”

Insertion sort runs in $\Theta(n+d)$ where $d$ is the number of inversions in the input. For nearly-sorted arrays ($d=O(n)$) it beats $O(n\log n)$ algorithms in practice.

Lower bound on comparison sorts

Any comparison-based sort requires $\Omega(n\log n)$ comparisons in the worst case (decision-tree argument: $n!$ leaves → depth $\ge \log_2 n! = \Theta(n\log n)$). All three iterative sorts here are $\Theta(n^2)$ — they do not meet this lower bound, which motivates the $O(n\log n)$ divide-and-conquer sorts in Unit 3.

Stability — why it matters

When records have multiple keys (e.g. sort by date, then by name), a stable sort preserves the prior ordering of equal keys, allowing multi-criteria sorting via successive passes.

Common pitfalls

• Forgetting the early-termination flag makes bubble sort lose its $\Theta(n)$ best case.
• Selection sort is "the same" regardless of input — no best-case speed-up.
• Recursive Fibonacci is the textbook example of overlapping sub-problems — mention this when motivating dynamic programming.

Viva quick-fire

Q: Why is Euclid faster than checking every divisor? A: The mod operation shrinks the input by a factor of at least 2 every two steps → $O(\log n)$ instead of $O(\sqrt n)$.
Q: Which sort would you use for an almost sorted array? A: Insertion sort — $\Theta(n+d)$.
Q: Which sort minimises the number of writes? A: Selection sort — only $n-1$ swaps.
Q: Is the iterative Fibonacci asymptotically optimal? A: For pure addition arithmetic, yes — $\Theta(n)$ additions. Matrix exponentiation reduces it to $\Theta(\log n)$ but with large constants.
Q: Why is bubble sort considered "educational only"? A: It has the worst constant factor among the three $\Theta(n^2)$ sorts.
