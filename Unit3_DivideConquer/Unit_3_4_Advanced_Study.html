🧠 Unit 3 — Advanced & Viva Edge

Lower bound on comparison sorts

Decision-tree argument: there are $n!$ permutations of the input and any comparison sort must produce a distinct leaf for each → tree height $\ge \log_2 n! = \Theta(n\log n)$. Merge / Heap meet this bound; quadratic sorts do not.

Why Build-Heap is $\Theta(n)$, not $\Theta(n\log n)$

Number of nodes at height $h$ in a heap of size $n$ is at most $\lceil n/2^{h+1}\rceil$. Heapify at height $h$ costs $O(h)$. Total: $\sum_{h=0}^{\log n} \lceil n/2^{h+1}\rceil h = O\big(n\sum_{h=0}^{\infty} h/2^h\big) = O(n)$, since $\sum h/2^h = 2$.

Partition schemes (Lomuto vs Hoare)

Lomuto: simpler, pivot at the end, one index walks forward. More swaps.
Hoare: two indices walk towards each other, fewer swaps, returns the split point rather than pivot's final position. Used in real-world libraries.

3-way partition (Dutch National Flag)

Splits into < pivot, = pivot, > pivot. Excellent for arrays with many duplicates — turns $\Theta(n^2)$ duplicate worst case into $\Theta(n)$.

Randomized algorithms — what "expected" means

Expected $\Theta(n\log n)$ is over the algorithm's random choices, NOT over the input. Worst input gives expected $\Theta(n\log n)$; no adversary can force quadratic behaviour by crafting input.

External sorting

When data doesn't fit in memory, Merge Sort wins because it accesses data sequentially. The k-way merge from disk runs in $O(n\log_k(n/M))$ I/Os.

Tail-call elimination in Quick Sort

Recurse on the smaller partition, iterate on the larger one → stack depth bounded by $O(\log n)$ even in the worst case.

Viva quick-fire

Q: Why is Quick Sort preferred over Merge Sort in practice? A: Smaller constant factors, in-place, cache-friendly.
Q: Why is Heap Sort not the universal choice? A: Poor cache locality compared with Quick / Merge.
Q: Can Quick Sort be made stable? A: Not without extra space.
Q: What is the minimum number of comparisons to find both min and max? A: $\lceil 3n/2 \rceil - 2$ — the MinMax algorithm achieves it.
Q: Is binary search faster than hashing? A: Binary search is $O(\log n)$; hashing $O(1)$ expected. Hashing wins unless ordering / range queries are also required.
